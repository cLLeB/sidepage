The Living Cell

A short guide to the smallest unit of life. Chapter 1

In one sentence: every living thing is made of cells, and every cell comes from another cell.

1. What a cell is

A cell is a tiny, self-contained unit that takes in food, turns it into energy, responds to its surroundings and makes copies of itself. Some living things, such as bacteria, are a single cell. A human body is made of around thirty trillion cells of more than two hundred kinds, from long nerve cells to flat skin cells.

Cells are small because of a simple rule of geometry. As a cell grows, its volume increases faster than its surface. The surface is where food and oxygen come in and waste goes out, so a very large cell could not feed its own middle. Staying small keeps every part of the cell close to its surface.

2. The cell membrane

Every cell is wrapped in a membrane, a thin double layer of fat-like molecules with proteins set into it. The membrane decides what enters and leaves. Small molecules such as oxygen slip straight through; larger ones such as glucose need protein doors called channels and carriers.

3. Energy: the mitochondria

Mitochondria are the power stations of the cell. They break down sugar using oxygen and store the energy released in a molecule called ATP. A muscle cell, which needs a great deal of energy, can hold thousands of mitochondria.

Remember: respiration in the mitochondria turns glucose and oxygen into carbon dioxide, water and ATP.

4. Instructions: the nucleus

The nucleus holds the cell's DNA, the instructions for building every protein the cell needs. Copies of these instructions, written in a molecule called messenger RNA, leave the nucleus and are read by ribosomes, which join amino acids into proteins in the right order.

6. How cells divide

A cell makes two cells by copying its DNA and then splitting in two, a process called mitosis. Each new cell receives a full, identical set of instructions. This is how a body grows and how it repairs a cut.
